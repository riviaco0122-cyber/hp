Kinmedai: Japan’s golden-eye snapper and where to eat it

Kinmedai is the red, golden-eyed fish on sushi counters and ryokan tables across Japan. Here is what it is, how it is eaten and why the port of Shimoda is the place to try it.

By the Okaeri Japan team · Published 14 November 2026 · 3 min read

In short

  • Kinmedai is a deep-sea fish with red skin and large golden eyes. In English it is sold as splendid alfonsino or golden-eye snapper.
  • Shimoda, at the southern tip of the Izu Peninsula, is Japan’s top landing port for it, according to the city and Shizuoka Prefecture.
  • The classic dish is nitsuke, a whole fish simmered in sweet soy. A canteen inside the fish market opens at 7:00.
  • June is festival month. Winter is the other season locals name.

What is kinmedai?

Kinmedai is a deep-sea fish with red skin and large golden eyes, known in English as splendid alfonsino (Beryx splendens) or golden-eye snapper. It lives around 500 metres down (Tabi-mag). Shimoda’s city newsletter describes eyes that shine gold and skin that turns red all over about 40 minutes after the fish is landed (Shimoda City newsletter, June 2016).

The name is written 金目鯛, literally “golden-eye sea bream”, but kinmedai is neither a sea bream nor a snapper. The flesh is white and carries fat all year, which is why it works simmered, raw or seared.

Why is Shimoda the place to eat kinmedai?

Shimoda lands more kinmedai than any other port in Japan. Both the city and Shizuoka Prefecture describe it that way (Shimoda City newsletter, June 2016; Shizuoka Prefecture).

Menus in town often name one of two kinds. Jikinme is caught in nearby waters, between Oshima and Kozushima. Oki-kinme comes from fishing grounds further out (TV Tokyo, 2014). Ordering both side by side is the quickest way to taste the difference.

How is kinmedai eaten?

  • Nitsuke. A whole fish or a thick cut simmered in soy sauce and sugar. This is the dish most people come for.
  • Sashimi and aburi. Raw slices, or slices with the skin seared.
  • Shabu-shabu. Thin slices swished through hot broth at the table.
  • Everything else. The local tourism association also lists kinmedai ramen, burgers and croquettes (Tabi-mag).

Where can you try kinmedai by the harbour?

Ichiba no Shokudo Kinmetei is a canteen inside the Shimoda fish market, about ten minutes on foot from Izukyu-Shimoda Station. Shizuoka Prefecture lists its hours as 7:00 to 9:30 for the morning set and 11:00 to 15:00 for lunch, closed on Tuesdays (Shizuoka Prefecture, page updated March 2025). Hours change, so check before you walk over.

When is the best time to eat kinmedai?

There are two answers. Winter is often called the season. The city newsletter adds that June, just before spawning, is said to be when the fish is fattiest (Shimoda City newsletter, June 2016).

June is also the Shimoda Kinme Matsuri, which runs from 1 to 30 June every year. During the festival you can watch the landings from a viewing area beside the market, roughly 7:30 to 9:00. Boats do not go out on Tuesdays or Saturdays, or after rough seas (Tabi-mag).

Why stay overnight instead of visiting for the day?

The market does its business early, and the direct Odoriko limited express from Tokyo takes about 2 hours 35 minutes (Ekitan). A day trip arrives after the boats have unloaded. Staying in town puts you at the harbour for the morning landings and at the canteen when it opens. Routes and fares are in our guide to getting to the Izu Peninsula from Tokyo.

Our home in Shimoda’s old town, NODE Shimoda, is about ten minutes on foot from the station. It has a kitchen with proper cooking equipment and a supermarket within walking distance, so you can cook what you find.

Common questions

Is kinmedai the same as red snapper?

No. Kinmedai is an alfonsino, a different family of fish from the snappers. The English name golden-eye snapper is a market name.

What does kinmedai taste like?

Kinmedai has white flesh that carries fat all year, so it stays rich whether it is simmered, seared or eaten raw. The classic preparation is nitsuke, simmered in soy sauce and sugar.

Where is kinmedai caught?

Shimoda, on the Izu Peninsula in Shizuoka Prefecture, lands more kinmedai than any other port in Japan. The fish sold there as jikinme is caught in nearby waters between Oshima and Kozushima.

How do you say kinmedai?

Roughly “kin-meh-dye”. Locals shorten it to kinme.
