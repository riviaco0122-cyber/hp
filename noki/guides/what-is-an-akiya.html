What is an akiya? Japan’s empty houses, and what buying one really means

An akiya is just a house nobody lives in. Japan counted about 9 million in 2023, and fewer than half are the abandoned kind the headlines mean.

By the Noki team · Published 21 November 2026 · 8 min read

In short

  • Japan had about 9 million empty homes in 2023, a record 13.8% of all housing.
  • Just over half were waiting for a tenant or buyer; about 3.9 million were simply left unused.
  • You can buy one from abroad, and town-run akiya banks list many, but repairs and fees come on top.

If you’ve seen headlines about Japan giving houses away, the real picture is less dramatic and a lot more useful. An akiya (空き家, literally “empty house”) is simply a house that nobody lives in. Most of them aren’t ruins, and we think a low asking price tells you less than you’d hope about what you’d end up paying.

What does “akiya” actually mean?

In everyday Japanese, it just means a house where nobody lives. The catch is that Japan counts akiya in two different ways, and the news nearly always uses the wider one.

The wider one comes from a national housing survey held every five years (the Housing and Land Survey). It counts homes where no household usually lives on the survey day1. So flats between tenants, houses on the market and holiday homes all count. So do homes left empty after someone moved into hospital, and homes that are about to be pulled down1.

The narrower one comes from a law on empty houses, often called the Vacant Houses Act. It covers buildings that normally aren’t lived in or used, along with their land2. The government’s guideline treats about a year with no sign of use as one yardstick3. That law is what lets a town step in when an empty house starts to fall apart. So when you see a huge akiya number in the news, it’s the survey’s count, and plenty of those homes are simply waiting for the next tenant.

How many akiya are there, and how many are really abandoned?

About 9 million, a record. The ones people mean by “abandoned” are about 3.9 million of those4. The rest all have a purpose: just over half are for rent or for sale, and a small share are second homes.

The left-unused group is the one that keeps growing, and it’s mostly detached houses. About four in five empty detached houses fall into it4. So only a thin slice of Japan’s empty homes were actually for sale when the survey was taken.

Why are so many houses left empty?

Mostly because they were inherited, and leaving a house as it is can feel easier than deciding what to do with it. About 58% of the empty homes in a 2024 government survey of owners had been inherited5. Many of them became empty when the person living there died5.

It isn’t usually that the owners live far away, either. About eight in ten empty homes are within an hour of the owner’s own home6. When owners who plan to keep a house empty were asked why, the most common answers were these6:

  • they use it to store things
  • they don’t want to pay for demolition
  • the house is too old
  • there’d be no use for the land even if the house came down

Some also worry that pulling the house down would raise their property tax6. The law is nudging things along, though. Under the current law, heirs must register an inherited property within three years of learning about it7. We think that should make it easier to find out who owns an empty house. The upshot for you is that plenty of akiya owners aren’t trying to sell. They’re waiting, and a house that isn’t listed anywhere may still have an owner who’d talk.

Are akiya only in the countryside?

No. Tokyo has more empty homes than any other prefecture, but only about 214,000 of them are the left-unused kind (2.6% of homes)4. The share of homes standing empty is highest in rural prefectures.

The difference shows up in the left-unused group. In Wakayama and Tokushima, those homes make up over 12% of all housing4. So the forgotten houses that end up cheap are mostly in the countryside, even if the biggest raw numbers are in the cities.

Is an akiya really cheap?

The asking price can be very low, but in our view the total rarely is. Repairs, safety checks, fees and taxes all come on top, and they vary so much from one house to the next that only written quotes tell you the real figure.

We think that last row also gives some owners a reason to sell. Since an amendment in December 2023, a house left to decay can cost its owner more in tax once the town steps in12.

Our place in Shimoda is a rented flat, not an akiya we bought, but it gives you a rough idea of the numbers. When we turned that older flat into a guesthouse, the building work alone came to about ¥4.1 million. Furniture, appliances and supplies added roughly ¥2.4 million, and it took about seven months to get ready.

A whole house is usually a much bigger job, so we wouldn’t trust any asking price until we’d seen a builder’s quote. If you’re seriously thinking about it, we’d suggest living in the town for a month before you buy.

Where do you find an akiya for sale?

Mostly through akiya banks, lists of empty homes that towns and cities put together, though ordinary estate agents sell them too. The government has a national akiya bank, run by two property companies, LIFULL and At Home, where you can search many towns’ listings in one place13.

It doesn’t cover everywhere, though. As of October 2026, more than 1,100 towns and cities take part14, so if a town you like isn’t on it, its own website is worth a look. Each bank also sets its own rules. Shizuoka City’s main bank, for example, only covers homes in the city’s designated urban area, and only estate agents can list properties on it; homes in its hill and mountain districts go on a separate bank15.

A few towns go further. Kamiichi, a town in Toyama, runs a zero-yen akiya bank where owners give away the house and land for free16. The town only passes on the information, though. You deal with the owner directly and pay for the registration, repairs, moving and taxes yourself11.

If you want to start looking, this is the order we’d go in:

  1. Search the national akiya bank and narrow it down by region.
  2. Look up the akiya bank page of any town you like, since it may list homes the national site doesn’t.
  3. Read who can use that bank, because the rules change from town to town.
  4. Contact the town office about the houses that interest you.
  5. Go and see the house, and the town, in person.

For that last step, renting a place by the month gives you time to see more than one house without rushing.

Can you buy one if you live outside Japan?

Yes. Living abroad doesn’t stop you buying, though there’s a little more paperwork than there used to be. Since April 2026, non-residents who buy property in Japan, even a home to live in, have had to report it to the Ministry of Finance afterwards17,18. And since October 2026, every buyer, Japanese or not, gives their nationality when the purchase is registered19.

The rules are still moving. A land bill before the Diet would add new limits in some areas20, but it hadn’t passed as of November 202621. We’d also treat a house and a visa as separate questions, so our guide to how long you can stay in Japan is worth reading alongside this. Our guide to living in the town before you buy goes into the buying rules in more detail.

Common questions

Is an akiya the same as an abandoned house?

No. In Japan’s housing survey, “akiya” also covers homes for rent or sale and second homes. The ones closest to abandoned are the homes left unused, about 3.9 million of the 9 million counted4.

Why don’t owners just sell or knock them down?

Many inherited the house and haven’t decided what to do with it. Owners who plan to keep one empty most often say they use it for storage, don’t want to pay for demolition, think it’s too old, or see no use for the land if it were cleared. Some also worry that pulling it down would raise their property tax6.

Are there free akiya in Japan?

Yes, a few. Kamiichi in Toyama, for example, runs a zero-yen akiya bank where owners give houses away16. You still pay for registration, repairs, moving and taxes yourself11.

Is an old akiya safe in an earthquake?

It depends on the house. Homes built to the standard used before June 1981 often aren’t strong enough, so it’s sensible to get one assessed before you buy. National and local governments help pay for assessments and strengthening work8,9.
