Buying an akiya in Japan? Live in one for a month first

Japan has about nine million empty homes, and as of November 2026 foreigners aren’t banned from buying one. But the house is the easy part. The town is what you’re really signing up for, and a month there tells you more than any listing.

By the Noki team · Updated 15 November 2026 · 9 min read

In short

  • An akiya is an empty house. Japan had about 9.0 million of them on 1 October 2023, a record 13.8% of all homes.
  • As of November 2026, foreigners aren’t banned from buying a home, but there’s more paperwork than before. We wouldn’t count on a house to help with a visa.
  • A month in the town shows you what a listing can’t: the winter cold, whether you need a car, where you’ll shop and how the neighbours are.
  • As of November 2026, most visa-exempt visitors can stay up to 90 days, and remote workers from certain countries can apply for a six-month digital nomad status.
  • For somewhere to stay, you’ve got a town’s trial home, a monthly rental or a guesthouse with monthly rates. From abroad, the last two are usually more realistic.

An akiya (空き家) is simply a house that nobody lives in. Japan’s 2023 Housing and Land Survey counted 9.0 million of them on 1 October 2023, a record 13.8% of the country’s homes (Statistics Bureau of Japan). About 3.86 million weren’t for rent or sale and weren’t anyone’s second home either, up by 369,000 since 2018 (Statistics Bureau of Japan). That’s the group people usually mean when they talk about Japan’s abandoned houses.

Can foreigners buy an akiya?

Yes. As of November 2026, Japan doesn’t ban foreigners or non-residents from buying a home (Real Estate Japan; Ministry of Finance (FAQ)). There’s more paperwork than before, though. Since April 2026, non-residents who buy property have to report it under the Foreign Exchange Act, even a home they plan to live in (Ministry of Finance; Ministry of Finance (FAQ)). Since 5 October 2026, every buyer, Japanese or not, gives their nationality when the purchase is registered (Ministry of Justice).

The rules are still moving. As of November 2026, a bill before the Diet would create new zones within about 1 km of some defence and coast guard sites, and on uninhabited border islands (Cabinet Office; Cabinet Office (bill outline)). Every transfer of land ownership in those zones would need the government’s approval in advance, and the law would take effect within 18 months of being promulgated (Cabinet Office (bill outline)).

We’d also treat a house and a visa as two separate questions. If you’re hoping that owning a home will help you stay in Japan longer, ask an administrative scrivener (gyoseishoshi) who handles visas before you count on it. With the rules changing this fast, we think it’s sensible to have a lawyer or judicial scrivener (shiho shoshi) look at your own case before you sign.

Why live in the town before you buy?

Buying the house is the easy part. What you’re really committing to is the town, and a listing can only tell you so much: the price, the floor plan, a few photos. The things that decide whether you’ll be happy there only show up once you’ve lived there for a while.

What you want to knowHow a month shows you
How cold an old wooden house gets in January, and what heating costsYou’ll feel it on the first cold night and see it on the electricity bill.
Whether daily life works without a carA week of shopping and errands on foot, by bus or by bike makes it obvious. Our house in Shimoda is about ten minutes’ walk from the station, with a supermarket, a convenience store and places to eat nearby, and that kind of spot is easy to test.
Where you’ll buy food, and when the shops closeCooking most of your own meals soon shows you what’s easy to find and what isn’t.
How rubbish sorting works on that streetYou’ll be following the local collection calendar like everyone else. If there’s an English version, it’s worth asking city hall for it.
Whether the internet holds up for a working dayNormal working hours, video calls included, are the real test.
What humid months and typhoons do to the buildingNeighbours and local builders know what tends to fail first in houses like it.
How the neighbourhood treats a newcomerSaying hello, joining a clean-up day if there is one, and asking how other newcomers settled in will tell you a lot. Neighbours notice small things, too. When our broadband was being installed in Shimoda, we heard from neighbours about where the engineer’s van was parked.

None of this comes up in a viewing.

What only shows up once you live in an old house?

The wiring and the internet, in our experience. We learnt this when one of our founding members rented an old house in Shimoda from its owner and turned it into a guesthouse. We rented rather than bought, but we think a buyer would run into the same things.

The old two-wire electrics couldn’t run the air conditioner, the fridge and the microwave all at once, so we had to ask guests not to use them together. Upgrading the wiring costs real money, so it’s something to find out before you budget, not after. Getting broadband put in took more than two months because it was the busy season, and we got by on a plug-in home router until then.

That’s true of cheap houses in general. The asking price is only part of the bill: renovation, taxes, insurance and upkeep come on top, and they vary so much from one building to the next that only written quotes will tell you the real figure.

How long can you stay in Japan to test a town?

Long enough for a proper trial, for most people. There are two main ways to do it without a work or residence visa, and the rules below are as of November 2026.

Way to stayHow longWho it’s for
Short-term visitor, visa-exemptUp to 90 days for most visa-exempt nationalities. Some get 15 or 30 days, and some passports come with extra conditions (Ministry of Foreign Affairs of Japan).Anyone from a visa-exempt country or region. The rule for your own passport is the one that counts.
Digital nomad statusSix months, and it can’t be renewed. Once six months have passed after you leave Japan, you can come back under it again (Immigration Services Agency).Nationals of countries and regions that are both visa-exempt and have a tax treaty with Japan (Immigration Services Agency (country list)). You need an annual income of at least ¥10 million and private medical insurance with at least ¥10 million of treatment cover, and you can’t work for a Japanese employer (Immigration Services Agency).

This is general information, not immigration advice. The list of eligible countries for the digital nomad status changes, so check it for your own passport.

Where can you stay for a month to test a town?

You’ve got three main choices: a town’s trial home, a monthly rental, or a guesthouse with monthly rates. Which one works depends on the town, and on where you’re coming from.

OptionHow longGood to know
A town’s trial home (otameshi jutaku)From a day or two up to a few months, and in one city up to two years (Shizuoka City; Minami-Alps City; Shizuoka City (long-term))Each town sets its own rules, mostly for people planning to move. Minami-Alps City lets one for one to three months at ¥21,000 a month (February 2026) (Minami-Alps City).
A monthly rentalA month or moreA furnished room or flat let by the month. In a small town there may be few to choose from, so it helps to search early.
A guesthouse or holiday home with monthly ratesA week to a month or moreOften the easiest to book from abroad, and you can try living in an older house without committing to anything.

Trial homes sound ideal, but there’s a catch: they’re built for people who are moving, not for people testing a town from abroad. Minami-Alps City’s home is for people registered as living outside Yamanashi who intend to move to the city and have signed up for a relocation consultation (Minami-Alps City). Nationality isn’t one of the listed conditions, so if you live overseas, the city’s relocation desk can tell you whether you’d qualify.

Some cities spell it out. Shizuoka City’s long-term trial flats are city-run apartments you can rent for six months to under two years, but only if you live outside Shizuoka Prefecture and have decided to move to the city. Foreign nationals qualify only as permanent residents, long-term residents, special permanent residents or spouses of Japanese nationals (page updated August 2026) (Shizuoka City (long-term)). So a tourist or digital nomad stay won’t qualify there. In our view, if you’re coming from abroad, a monthly rental or a guesthouse is usually the realistic choice. If you’ve set your heart on a trial home, the town’s relocation office is the first call to make.

It’s an older house in the old town, renovated for long stays and licensed under Japan’s Hotel Business Act. Most of our guests so far have come from Europe and North America, and some come to work remotely.

What does a trial month look like?

Mostly, it looks like ordinary life, with a few deliberate tests built in. Here’s roughly how we’d spend it, based on what caught us out in Shimoda.

The first week goes on settling in: the supermarket, the clinic, the bus stop and the rubbish calendar. If you’re staying in an older house, ask your host what amperage the electricity contract allows and where the breaker box is. Our own old wiring couldn’t run the air conditioner, fridge and microwave together, and those two answers give you a feel for what an old house can handle.

By the second week you’re working your usual hours and cooking at home. That’s when you find out whether the connection survives a day of video calls, and you’ll see what heating or cooling really costs. It’s also a good moment to ask a local provider how long a new line takes there, since ours took more than two months.

The third week is for asking around. You might visit the city hall desk that handles empty houses, talk to a local agent, or ask a builder roughly what rewiring an old house costs. Neighbours are often the best people to tell you what humid summers and typhoons do to houses like theirs.

By the fourth week you’ll have a fair idea of what would need to change, about the house and about the way you live. Writing those costs and doubts down while they’re fresh makes it much easier to compare towns, or to walk away.

Common questions

Can foreigners buy property in Japan?

As of November 2026 there’s no ban, but non-residents must report a purchase under the Foreign Exchange Act, and every buyer gives their nationality at registration (Ministry of Finance; Ministry of Justice). The rules are changing, so a lawyer or judicial scrivener can check your own case.

Will owning a house in Japan help me get a visa?

We wouldn’t count on it. We treat a house and a visa as separate questions, so if staying longer is part of your plan, ask an administrative scrivener (gyoseishoshi) who handles visas before you buy.

Is a cheap house in Japan really cheap?

Not always. The purchase price is only one part of the bill. Renovation, taxes, insurance and upkeep come on top, and they vary a lot from one building to the next, so written quotes are the only way to know before you commit.

Can I use a town’s trial home as a foreigner?

It depends on the town. Trial homes are mostly for people who plan to move there, and some spell out who qualifies. As of August 2026, Shizuoka City’s long-term trial flats took foreign nationals only with permanent, long-term or special permanent residence, or as the spouse of a Japanese national (Shizuoka City (long-term)). The town’s relocation office will know the current rules.

How many akiya are there in Japan?

About 9.0 million homes were empty on 1 October 2023, 13.8% of all housing, according to the 2023 Housing and Land Survey (Statistics Bureau of Japan).
