Buying an akiya in Japan? Live in one for a month first

Japan had about nine million empty homes in 2023, and non-residents can buy one. But you’re buying into a town, and a month there tells you more than any listing.

By the Noki team · Updated 16 November 2026 · 10 min read

In short

  • Since April 2026, non-residents who buy a home have had to report it to the Ministry of Finance.
  • A month shows you whether you need a car, how the neighbours are and what heating or cooling really costs.
  • From abroad, a monthly rental or a guesthouse with monthly rates is usually more realistic than a town’s trial home.

An akiya (空き家) is simply a house that nobody lives in, and Japan has a record number of them: about 9 million in the 2023 housing survey, or 13.8% of all homes1. Plenty of those are just waiting for a tenant or a buyer, though1. In our experience, when people talk about Japan’s abandoned houses, they mean the 3.86 million that aren’t for rent, for sale or anyone’s second home. That group is still growing: there are more of them now than in 20181.

Can foreigners buy an akiya?

Yes, non-residents can buy. What’s changed is the paperwork, and the Ministry of Finance’s rules spell it out. Since April 2026, if you buy property in Japan while living abroad, you have to report it to the ministry under the Foreign Exchange Act, even if it’s a home you plan to live in2,3. Real Estate Japan, a property site for foreign buyers, puts it simply: you don’t need to live in Japan to buy4. And since 5 October 2026, every buyer, Japanese or not, gives their nationality when the purchase is registered5.

The rules are still moving, though. A bill before the Diet would create new zones within about 1 km of some defence and coast guard sites, and on uninhabited border islands6,7. Inside those zones, every transfer of land ownership would need the government’s approval in advance. The changes would take effect within 18 months of the act being promulgated7. As of mid-November 2026, the bill was still before the House of Representatives, so it isn’t law yet8. If a house you like is near one of those sites, that bill is the one to keep an eye on.

We’d also treat a house and a visa as two separate questions. If you’re hoping that owning a home will help you stay in Japan longer, it’s worth checking with an administrative scrivener (gyoseishoshi) who handles visas before you count on it. And with the rules changing this fast, we think it’s sensible to have a lawyer or judicial scrivener (shiho shoshi) look at your own case before you sign.

Why live in the town before you buy?

Buying the house is the easy part. What you’re really committing to is the town, and a listing can only tell you so much: the price, the floor plan, a few photos. The things that decide whether you’ll be happy there only show up once you’ve lived there for a while.

What you want to knowHow a month shows you
How cold an old wooden house gets in January, and what heating costsYou’ll feel it on the first cold night and see it on the electricity bill.
Whether daily life works without a carA week of shopping and errands on foot, by bus or by bike makes it obvious, especially if you try the trips that aren’t on your doorstep, too.
Where you’ll buy food, and when the shops closeCooking most of your own meals soon shows you what’s easy to find and what isn’t.
How rubbish sorting works on that streetYou’ll be following the local collection calendar like everyone else. If there’s an English version, it’s worth asking city hall for it.
Whether the internet holds up for a working dayNormal working hours, video calls included, are the real test.
What humid months and typhoons do to the buildingNeighbours and local builders know what tends to fail first in houses like it.
How the neighbourhood treats a newcomerSaying hello, joining a clean-up day if there is one, and asking how other newcomers settled in will tell you a lot. Neighbours notice small things, too. When our broadband was being installed in Shimoda, we heard from neighbours about where the engineer’s van was parked.

None of this comes up in a viewing.

What only shows up once you live in an old house?

The wiring and the internet, in our experience. We learnt this when one of our founding members rented an older flat in Shimoda from its owner and turned it into a guesthouse. We rented rather than bought, but we think a buyer would run into the same things.

The old two-wire electrics couldn’t run the air conditioner, the fridge and the microwave all at once, so we had to ask guests not to use them together. Upgrading the wiring costs real money, so it’s something to find out before you budget, not after. Getting broadband put in took more than two months because it was the busy season, and we got by on a plug-in home router until then.

The wiring and the wait for broadband are small examples of a bigger point. With a cheap old house, the asking price is only part of what you’ll pay. Renovation, taxes, insurance and upkeep come on top, and they vary so much from one building to the next that only written quotes will tell you the real figure.

Which ways of staying let you try out a town properly?

For most people, there are two: a visa-free short stay and the six-month digital nomad status. Neither needs a work visa or a move to Japan, and both give you plenty of time to settle into a town and see how it suits you. The rules below are as of November 2026.

Way to stayHow longWho it’s for
Short-term visitor, visa-exemptUp to 90 days for most visa-exempt nationalities, though some are given 15, 30 or 180 days9.Anyone from a visa-exempt country or region. You can’t do paid work on this status, and the rule for your own passport is the one that counts9.
Digital nomad statusSix months, and it can’t be renewed. You can apply for it again six months after you leave Japan10.Nationals of countries and regions that are both visa-exempt and have a tax treaty with Japan11. You need an annual income of at least ¥10 million and private medical insurance with at least ¥10 million of treatment cover, and you can’t work for a Japanese employer10.

If the digital nomad route fits you, our guide to Japan’s digital nomad visa has more on who qualifies and how it works.

For extensions, longer statuses and what happens if you leave and come back, see our guide to how long you can stay in Japan.

This is general information, not immigration advice. The list of eligible countries for the digital nomad status changes, so check it for your own passport.

Where can you stay for a month to test a town?

You’ve got three main choices: a town’s trial home, a monthly rental, or a guesthouse with monthly rates. Which one works depends on the town, and on where you’re coming from.

OptionHow longGood to know
A town’s trial home (otameshi jutaku)From a day or two up to a few months, and in one city for up to just under two years12,13,14Each town sets its own rules, mostly for people planning to move. Minami-Alps City rents its trial home for one to three months at ¥21,000 a month (February 2026)13.
A monthly rentalA month or moreA furnished room or flat let by the month. In a small town there may be few to choose from, so it helps to search early.
A guesthouse or holiday home with monthly ratesA week to a month or moreOften the easiest to book from abroad, and you can try daily life in the town without committing to anything.

Trial homes sound ideal, but there’s a catch: they’re built for people who are moving, not for people testing a town from abroad. Minami-Alps City’s home is for people registered as living outside Yamanashi who intend to move to the city and have signed up for a relocation consultation13. Nationality isn’t one of the listed conditions, so if you live overseas, the city’s relocation desk can tell you whether you’d qualify.

Some cities spell it out. Shizuoka City’s long-term trial flats are city-run apartments you can rent for six months to under two years. They’re only for people who live outside Shizuoka Prefecture and have decided, or are preparing, to move to the city. If you’re not Japanese, you also need permanent or long-term residence, special permanent residence, or to be the spouse or child of a Japanese national (August 2026)14. So a tourist or digital nomad stay won’t get you in.

That’s why, if you’re coming from abroad, we think a monthly rental or a guesthouse is usually the realistic choice. Our guide to monthly rentals in Japan explains how they work. If you’ve set your heart on a trial home, the town’s relocation office is the first call to make.

Shimoda City does it differently. Under rules in force since April 2026, it pays half the rate at designated hotels for people thinking of moving there15. The help covers up to 10 nights, at up to ¥4,000 a night15. The catch is that you need to be registered as living in Japan outside Shizuoka Prefecture, so it won’t help if you’re coming from abroad15.

Towns within easy reach of Tokyo are the simplest to test, because getting there and back is easy. Shimoda, on the Izu Peninsula, is one of them: direct trains from Tokyo Station run every day and take about two and a half hours (2 hours 29 to 44 minutes on the November 2026 timetable)16, and our guide to getting to the Izu Peninsula from Tokyo has the details.

If Shimoda is on your list, we run a small place there called NODE Shimoda, which can be a base while you get a feel for the town. It’s a renovated flat of about 40 m² in an apartment building in the old town, 8 minutes’ walk from Izukyu-Shimoda Station17,18. Our site describes it as handy for medium and long stays19, and it’s licensed under Japan’s Hotel Business Act. Most of our guests so far have come from Europe and North America, and some come to work remotely.

What does a trial month look like?

Mostly, it looks like ordinary life, with a few deliberate tests built in. Based on what caught us out in Shimoda, we’d plan it roughly week by week.

The first week goes on settling in: the supermarket, the clinic, the bus stop and the rubbish calendar. If you’re staying in an older building, it’s worth asking your host what amperage the electricity contract allows and where the breaker box is. The old wiring in our Shimoda flat couldn’t run the air conditioner, fridge and microwave at the same time. The amperage tells you roughly how much you can run at once, and knowing where the breaker box is turns a tripped switch into a quick fix.

By the second week you’re working your usual hours and cooking at home. That’s when you find out whether the connection survives a day of video calls, and you’ll see what heating or cooling really costs. It’s also a good moment to ask a local provider how long a new line takes there, since ours took more than two months.

The third week is for asking around. You might visit the city hall desk that handles empty houses, talk to a local agent, or ask a builder roughly what rewiring an old house costs. Neighbours are often the best people to tell you what humid summers and typhoons do to houses like theirs.

By the fourth week you’ll have a fair idea of what would need to change, about the house and about the way you live. Writing those costs and doubts down while they’re fresh makes it much easier to compare towns, or to walk away.

Common questions

Can foreigners buy property in Japan?

Yes. Non-residents can buy, and since April 2026 they’ve had to report the purchase to the Ministry of Finance under the Foreign Exchange Act. Every buyer also gives their nationality at registration2,5. The rules are changing, so a lawyer or judicial scrivener can check your own case.

Will owning a house in Japan help me get a visa?

We wouldn’t count on it. We treat a house and a visa as separate questions, so if staying longer is part of your plan, ask an administrative scrivener (gyoseishoshi) who handles visas before you buy.

Is a cheap house in Japan really cheap?

Not always. The purchase price is only one part of the bill. Renovation, taxes, insurance and upkeep come on top. They vary a lot from one building to the next, so written quotes are the only way to know before you commit.

Can I use a town’s trial home as a foreigner?

It depends on the town. Trial homes are mostly for people who plan to move there, and some spell out who qualifies. As of August 2026, Shizuoka City’s long-term trial flats took foreign nationals only with permanent, long-term or special permanent residence, or as the spouse or child of a Japanese national14. The town’s relocation office will know the current rules.

How many akiya are there in Japan?

About 9.0 million, according to the 2023 Housing and Land Survey1. That’s a record 13.8% of all housing.
