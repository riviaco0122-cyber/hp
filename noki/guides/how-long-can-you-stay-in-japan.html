How long can you stay in Japan? 90 days, and the ways to stay longer

Visa-free visitors usually get up to 90 days, though some nationalities may get 15, 30 or 180. For six months or a year, pick the right status before you come.

By the Noki team · Published 20 November 2026 · 8 min read

In short

  • Visa-free stays are usually up to 90 days, though JNTO notes some nationalities may get 15, 30 or 180.
  • Extensions need good reason, and every time you come back, you’re checked again at the border.
  • For six months or a year, look at the digital nomad status or the long stay for sightseeing.

Ask online how long you can stay in Japan and you’ll get answers from 90 days to a year, sometimes in the same thread. The real answer depends on two things: the passport you hold and the status you enter on. Everything below describes the rules as of November 2026.

How long can you stay in Japan without a visa?

Usually up to 90 days. JNTO says most short-term stays allow up to 90 days, and notes that some nationalities may be granted 15, 30 or 180 days1. Officially, you enter as a Temporary Visitor.

Which one you get comes down to your passport. JNTO points to the Ministry of Foreign Affairs list of visa-exempt countries and regions, and that list is the one to trust for your own case1. If your passport isn’t on it, you’ll need a visa before you travel.

The status is meant for short stays: sightseeing, recreation, sport, visiting relatives, attending meetings and business liaison, among other things2. It doesn’t cover paid work, long-term study or applying to live here1.

Can you extend a visa-free stay?

You can apply, but we wouldn’t plan a trip around it. Japan’s Immigration Control Act lets you apply to renew your period of stay, and the Minister of Justice can grant it only when there are reasonable grounds2.

Timing matters if your plans fall apart near the end. Say you apply before your period of stay ends and there’s no decision yet. You can then stay until the decision or two months after your old expiry date, whichever comes first2. That grace period doesn’t apply if you were given 30 days or less2. So if you think you’ll go over, it’s worth talking to the immigration authorities before the date, not after.

Switching to another status from inside Japan is just as narrow. For a short-term visitor, the Act only allows a change of status when there are unavoidable special circumstances2. That’s why we’d sort out any longer status before you fly.

Can you leave Japan and come back for another 90 days?

You can come back, but a new entry isn’t a fresh 90 days by right. Each time you land, an immigration inspector checks that your passport is valid and that what you plan to do here is genuine and fits your status. They also check that the period fits the rules2. It’s up to you to show that you meet those conditions2.

People often ask how many days they need to spend abroad before coming back, or whether there’s a limit per year. What the law sets out is the check itself. At every entry, the inspector decides on the landing conditions in Article 7 of the Act, such as whether the purpose of your stay is genuine2. We don’t know of a number of days that makes the next entry safe, so we wouldn’t build a plan on leaving and coming back again and again.

In our view, the better plan for anything beyond 90 days is to pick a status that fits the length of your stay, before you come.

Which status fits six months or a year?

If you aren’t taking a job here, there are two main routes past 90 days. One is the digital nomad status, for up to six months. The other is a long stay for sightseeing and recreation, for up to a year.

StatusHow longWho it’s forResidence card
Temporary Visitor, visa-exemptUsually up to 90 days. JNTO notes some nationalities may be granted 15, 30 or 180 days1Short stays such as sightseeing. No paid work1,2No2
Digital nomad (Designated Activities)Up to six months, with no extension3Remote workers with an employer or clients outside Japan who meet the income and insurance rules3No2,4
Long stay for sightseeing and recreation (Designated Activities)A stay of up to one year5People aged 18 or over staying for sightseeing and recreation, and a spouse who comes with them5Yes, if you’re given more than three months2,4

Study, work and family statuses work differently. For those, the law lets you apply in advance for a Certificate of Eligibility from the Minister of Justice, and someone at the school or company taking you on can apply for you2.

The digital nomad status suits people who want to keep working for an employer or clients abroad. It needs ¥10 million a year in income3, and our guide to Japan’s digital nomad visa covers who qualifies and what it’s like to live here without a residence card.

The long stay for sightseeing is less well known. It does come with a residence card for stays of more than three months, but it still doesn’t let you join National Health Insurance2,4,5, so you’ll want private health cover. Its other conditions, such as which passports qualify and what you need to show, are best checked with a Japanese embassy or consulate in your country.

For many people, though, 90 days is plenty for a first season. It’s enough to live in a town for a month or two before you buy a house. Our guide to renting a place by the month covers where you’d stay.

Can you work remotely while visiting Japan?

The law says a short-term visitor can’t run a business that brings in income or do activities they’re paid for without permission2. JNTO also says a tourist stay doesn’t allow paid work1. So we wouldn’t assume it’s fine just because the money comes from abroad. How the rule applies to your own job is a question for the immigration authorities.

Whether answering a few emails for your employer counts depends on what you actually do, and we won’t guess at it for you. What’s clear is that Japan has a status built for remote work. If you want to keep working for an employer or clients abroad while you stay longer, the digital nomad status is the one written for that3,4.

What happens if you overstay?

It’s serious, and it can keep you out of Japan for years. Staying past your period of stay without renewing or changing it is a ground for deportation2. It’s also a crime, with up to three years’ imprisonment, a fine of up to ¥3 million, or both2.

How long you’re kept out depends on how you leave. Here’s how the Act sets it out, in the version in force from 1 October 20262.

How you leaveHow long you can’t come back
Deported for the first timeFive years from your departure2
Deported after an earlier deportation or departure orderTen years2
Under a departure orderOne year. It’s five years if you only said you’d leave after an investigation began and you want to come back as a visitor2

A departure order is the gentler route, and it’s mostly in your hands. You may get one if you go to an immigration office yourself, intending to leave quickly. You also need a clean record: no past deportation or departure order, no listed criminal convictions, and a departure that’s sure to happen promptly2. If you realise you’ve gone over, going in early is far better than waiting.

Is anything changing for visa-free visitors?

Yes, but not yet. In June 2026, Japan promulgated an amendment to the Immigration Control Act, Act No. 32 of 2026, after it passed the Diet on 29 May6. It adds a system that lets visa-exempt short-term visitors give their details online in advance and be authorised by the Immigration Services Agency7.

The Japan Tourism Agency calls the system JESTA and says it’s planned to start in the 2028 financial year, which runs from April 2028 to March 20298. Under the Act, that part comes into force on a date set by cabinet order, no later than 31 March 2029, and as of November 2026 it isn’t in force7. Until then, nothing about it changes your trip.

All of this is general information as of November 2026, not immigration advice. For your own case, it’s best to check with a Japanese embassy or consulate, the Immigration Services Agency, or an administrative scrivener (gyoseishoshi) who handles visas.

Common questions

How long can I stay in Japan without a visa?

Usually up to 90 days. JNTO notes that some nationalities may be granted 15, 30 or 180 days1. The Ministry of Foreign Affairs list, which JNTO links to, is where to check yours1.

Can I extend my 90-day stay in Japan?

You can apply, but it’s only granted when there are reasonable grounds2. If you might go over, talk to the immigration authorities before your stay ends, not after.

Can I leave Japan and come back for another 90 days?

You can come back, but each entry is checked again. The inspector decides on the landing conditions, such as whether your plans are genuine and fit your status, and it’s up to you to show that2. We don’t know of a number of days abroad, or a yearly pattern, that makes the next entry safe, so we wouldn’t plan on going in and out.

Can I stay in Japan for six months or a year without a job?

There are two main routes. One is the digital nomad status, for up to six months with no extension3. The other is a long stay for sightseeing and recreation of up to a year, for people aged 18 or over5. A Japanese embassy or consulate can tell you whether you qualify.

Can I work remotely on a visa-free stay in Japan?

A short-term visitor can’t do activities they’re paid for without permission1,2. How that applies to your own job is a question for the immigration authorities. If you want to keep working for an employer or clients abroad for longer, the digital nomad status is built for that3.

What happens if I overstay in Japan?

Overstaying is a ground for deportation and a crime2. A first deportation keeps you out for five years, while leaving under a departure order usually means one year2.
