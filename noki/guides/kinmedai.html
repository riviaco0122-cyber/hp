Kinmedai: Japan’s golden-eye snapper and where to eat it

Kinmedai is the red, golden-eyed fish you may spot on a sushi menu or a ryokan dinner tray in Japan. It’s a deep-sea fish that’s often simmered in soy and sugar, and the port of Shimoda, which calls itself Japan’s top kinmedai port, is a good place to try it.

By the Noki team · Updated 16 November 2026 · 7 min read

In short

  • Kinmedai is a red deep-sea fish with big golden eyes, called splendid alfonsino in English. Whatever an English menu calls it, it isn’t a sea bream or a snapper, and it isn’t the same fish as nodoguro, which belongs to a different family and lives in shallower water.
  • The flesh is white and carries fat all year. You’ll see it simmered as nitsuke (in soy sauce and sugar), raw as sashimi and lightly seared. We’d start with nitsuke.
  • Catches off Tokyo, Chiba, Kanagawa and Shizuoka fell from just under 7,000 tonnes a year in 2005–2009 to 3,797 tonnes in 2020. A later national assessment found the breeding stock in 2023 above the target level and rising. We think deep-water fishing and smaller catches are why it isn’t cheap.
  • Shimoda, at the southern tip of the Izu Peninsula, describes itself as Japan’s top kinmedai port. The first train from Tokyo Station gets there too late for the morning landings. A 4:35 train from Shinagawa arrives just before 8:00, but that’s tight, so we’d stay the night before.

What is kinmedai?

Kinmedai is a deep-sea fish with red skin and big golden eyes, known in English as splendid alfonsino (Beryx splendens) (FishBase). It lives on continental slopes and on the sides and tops of underwater mountains, at around 200 to 800 metres off Chiba (Fisheries Agency, 2021; Chiba Prefecture, 2025). Shimoda’s city newsletter talks about eyes that shine gold and skin that turns red all over about 40 minutes after the fish is landed (Shimoda City newsletter, June 2016).

The name is written 金目鯛, which literally means “golden-eye sea bream”. If you’ve seen it called golden-eye snapper on an English menu, it’s the same fish, but it belongs to its own family, the alfonsinos (Berycidae), not the sea bream or snapper families (Tokyo Metropolitan Government).

What does kinmedai taste like, and how is it eaten?

Kinmedai is a rich white fish that carries plenty of fat, and it stays that way all year. Shimoda City puts that down to its life in deep water, and says it works in lots of different dishes (Shimoda City newsletter, June 2016). If you only try it once, we’d go for nitsuke, fish simmered in soy sauce and sugar, though in our view it’s just as good raw or lightly seared.

DishWhat it is
NitsukeA whole fish or a thick cut, simmered until the sauce turns glossy. Our pick for a first try, and we think a big fish is worth the extra: Shizuoka’s researchers, measuring kinmedai landed at Inatori in Izu (a 2007–2011 study), found that bigger fish carry more fat (Shizuoka Prefecture (fisheries research)).
Sashimi and aburiRaw slices, or slices with the skin lightly seared.
Shabu-shabuThin slices swished through hot broth at the table. If you see it on a menu, we’d give it a try.
Everything elseThe Japanese travel magazine Tabi-mag also lists kinmedai ramen, burgers and croquettes (Tabi-mag).

If you’re buying a whole fish, the same newsletter says a fresh one has clear golden eyes and bright red skin, and both turn whitish as it ages (Shimoda City newsletter, June 2016).

Why is kinmedai expensive?

We think it comes down to how it’s caught and how much is caught. Off the Kanto coast and the Izu Islands, kinmedai are caught mainly on hook-and-line gear, mostly vertical longlines (tatenawa) and bottom-set longlines (soko-tate-haenawa) lowered into deep water (Fisheries Agency, 2021; Chiba Prefecture, 2025). Catches off Tokyo, Chiba, Kanagawa and Shizuoka held at just under 7,000 tonnes a year in 2005–2009, then fell to 3,797 tonnes in 2020 (Fisheries Agency, 2021).

The official picture of the stock has shifted over the past few years. The Fisheries Agency’s 2021 assessment rated the stock low in 2020, with the breeding (spawning) stock flat over the previous five years (Fisheries Agency, 2021). Chiba Prefecture later reported that the national assessment found the 2023 spawning stock above its target level and increasing (Chiba Prefecture, 2025).

That target is the level that should allow the largest catch year after year without shrinking the stock. For you, it means kinmedai isn’t a fish to avoid, but it isn’t plentiful either. Chiba’s own landings were 1,467 tonnes in 2023, against 2,263 tonnes in 2007 (Chiba Prefecture, 2025).

How is kinmedai different from nodoguro?

Nodoguro is a different fish altogether: it’s the market name for akamutsu, known in English as blackthroat seaperch (Kyoto Prefecture; FishBase). The two are easy to mix up, since both are red and both are prized, but they sit in different families and even different orders, and nodoguro lives much shallower. If a menu offers both, you’re choosing between two different fish.

Is kinmedai safe to eat when you’re pregnant?

Because of mercury, Japan’s health ministry advises pregnant women to limit some fish to about one 80 g serving a week. Kinmedai is on that list, along with swordfish, bluefin tuna and bigeye tuna (Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare). The same advice puts one piece of sushi or sashimi at about 15 g, so 80 g is roughly five pieces. It’s meant to protect the unborn baby, not children or other adults (Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare). It’s general public guidance rather than medical advice, so if you’re pregnant, your own doctor or midwife is the person to ask.

Why is Shimoda the place to eat kinmedai?

Shimoda, at the southern tip of the Izu Peninsula, calls itself Japan’s top landing port for kinmedai, and Shizuoka Prefecture describes the town’s fish market the same way (Shimoda City newsletter, June 2016; Shizuoka Prefecture). In other words, you’re eating it where more of it is landed than anywhere else.

You may hear two local names. A 2014 TV Tokyo programme on Shimoda explained that jikinme are caught in nearby waters between Oshima and Kozushima, while oki-kinme come from grounds further out (TV Tokyo, 2014).

The easiest place to start is Ichiba no Shokudo Kinmetei, a canteen inside the Shimoda fish market about ten minutes on foot from Izukyu-Shimoda Station. Its menu includes a kinmedai rice bowl served three ways and a whole simmered kinmedai set. Shizuoka Prefecture lists its hours as 7:00 to 9:30 for the morning set and 11:00 to 15:00 for lunch, closed on Tuesdays (Shizuoka Prefecture, page updated March 2025). Hours do change, so a quick check before you walk over saves a wasted trip.

When is kinmedai in season?

Kinmedai is landed all year, but locals point to two times: winter, and around June, when the fish are fattiest.

WhenWhy
WinterThe traditional season, when kinmedai goes into hot pots (Shimoda City newsletter, June 2016; Shizuoka Prefecture (fisheries research)).
Around JuneBefore spawning, when the fish carry the most fat. In Shizuoka’s year of measuring fish from Inatori (part of a 2007–2011 study), the fat peaked in June at about 10% (Shimoda City newsletter, June 2016; Shizuoka Prefecture (fisheries research)).
Kinme Matsuri (all of June in 2016)In 2016 there was a viewing spot outside the fish market, open from about 7:30 to 9:00 a.m. except on Tuesdays and Saturdays (Shimoda City newsletter, June 2016).

The festival dates and viewing times can change from year to year, and bad weather can cancel the viewing (Shimoda City newsletter, June 2016). If you’re planning a trip around it, check that year’s details first.

Why stay overnight instead of visiting for the day?

You can just about reach the morning landings from Tokyo, but only on a very early train, so we’d stay the night before. The first Tokaido Line train from Tokyo Station leaves at 5:20 and doesn’t reach Atami until 7:08. There’s one earlier option: a 4:35 local from Shinagawa connects at Atami and gets to Izukyu-Shimoda at 7:59 (JR East timetable, November 2026) (JR East timetables). In 2016 the viewing ran from about 7:30 to 9:00 (Shimoda City newsletter, June 2016), so you’d catch the end at best.

Timing isn’t the only reason, though. In our view, a night in town gives you kinmedai for dinner and an unhurried morning: a walk down to the harbour, breakfast at the canteen, and no train to rush back for. Routes are in our guide to getting to the Izu Peninsula from Tokyo. If you’d like to cook a fish yourself, our home in the old town, NODE Shimoda, has a full kitchen and is about ten minutes on foot from the station.

Common questions

Is kinmedai the same as red snapper?

No. Kinmedai belongs to the alfonsino family (Berycidae), not the snapper or sea bream families (Tokyo Metropolitan Government). Whatever an English menu calls it, it isn’t a snapper.

Why is kinmedai so expensive?

We think it’s mainly how it’s caught and how much. It lives deep, at around 200 to 800 metres off Chiba, and is caught on hook-and-line gear, mostly vertical longlines (Fisheries Agency, 2021; Chiba Prefecture, 2025). Catches off Tokyo, Chiba, Kanagawa and Shizuoka fell from just under 7,000 tonnes a year in 2005–2009 to 3,797 tonnes in 2020 (Fisheries Agency, 2021). A later national assessment found the 2023 breeding stock above its target level and rising (Chiba Prefecture, 2025).

Is kinmedai the same as nodoguro?

No. Nodoguro is the market name for akamutsu, or blackthroat seaperch (FishBase), a red fish from a different family (Acropomatidae). It lives in shallower water, at around 80 to 200 metres depending on the area (Tokushima Prefecture; Ibaraki Prefecture).

Can I eat kinmedai when I’m pregnant?

Japan’s health ministry advises pregnant women to keep kinmedai to about one 80 g serving a week because of mercury, which is roughly five pieces of sushi (Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare). Your doctor or midwife can tell you what applies to you.

When is kinmedai in season?

It’s landed all year. Winter is the traditional season, and the fish are fattiest around June (Shimoda City newsletter, June 2016; Shizuoka Prefecture (fisheries research)).

Can I see the kinmedai landings on a day trip from Tokyo?

Only just. The first train from Tokyo Station is too late, and a 4:35 local from Shinagawa gets to Shimoda at 7:59 (JR East timetables), near the end of the 2016 viewing hours (Shimoda City newsletter, June 2016). We’d stay the night before, which also gives you a slow morning at the harbour.

How do you say kinmedai?

Roughly “kin-meh-dye”. Locals often shorten it to kinme.
